A reference for patients, students and carers

Every skin cancer is named for the cell it started in — and staged by how deep it went.

There is no single disease called skin cancer. There are roughly a dozen distinct tumours arising from different cells in the skin, each with its own appearance, growth pattern and outlook. This page sets them out in the order a pathologist would: by layer, by cell, by depth.

01 — Basis

Three questions decide the name and the treatment of any skin tumour.

How skin cancers are classified

Classification is not alphabetical or cosmetic. It follows three axes, and every named diagnosis is a point where those three axes meet.

Axis one

Cell of origin

Basal keratinocytes give basal cell carcinoma. Upper keratinocytes give squamous cell carcinoma. Pigment cells give melanoma. Touch-receptor cells give Merkel cell carcinoma. Connective tissue, blood vessels, sweat glands and immune cells each give their own rarer tumour.

Axis two

Behaviour

Some grow outward and destroy local tissue but almost never travel — basal cell carcinoma is the classic example. Others enter lymph and blood early. This is why a large basal cell carcinoma can be less dangerous than a small melanoma.

Axis three

Depth of invasion

In situ means the tumour has not crossed the basement membrane and cannot spread. Invasive means it has. For melanoma the depth is measured in millimetres (Breslow thickness) and drives the entire staging system.

The clinical shorthand: melanoma vs. non-melanoma

Doctors often split skin cancer into melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC), also called keratinocyte carcinoma. NMSC is far more common; melanoma causes most skin cancer deaths. The split is useful in conversation but hides the rarer tumours, which is why the modern grouping below is by cell of origin.

02 — Group one

Keratinocyte carcinomas. Roughly nine in ten skin cancers. Rarely fatal, frequently disfiguring if ignored.

Keratinocyte carcinomas

Tumours of the ordinary skin cells that make keratin. They arise almost entirely on sun-exposed sites and are the direct consequence of cumulative ultraviolet damage over decades.

Most common human cancer

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)

Arises from the basal layer at the bottom of the epidermis. Grows slowly over months to years, invades locally, and destroys cartilage and bone if left. Spread to other organs is extremely rare — under one in a thousand.

Sites
Nose, eyelid, cheek, ear, forehead, upper trunk
Look
Pearly or translucent bump with fine surface blood vessels; often a central dip that scabs, heals, and breaks down again
Tempo
Slow — a sore "that never quite heals" for a year or more
Spread
Very low
Treat
Excision, Mohs surgery on the face, curettage, topical therapy or radiotherapy for superficial disease
  • Nodular — the classic pearly dome; commonest form.
  • Superficial — flat red scaly plaque on the trunk, often mistaken for eczema.
  • Morphoeic / infiltrative — a pale scar-like patch with indistinct edges; far larger under the skin than it looks.
  • Pigmented — brown or blue-black flecks in a pearly nodule; commonly confused with melanoma.
  • Basosquamous — mixed features, behaves more aggressively than ordinary BCC.
Second commonest

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)

Arises from the flat keratin-producing cells higher in the epidermis. Grows faster than BCC and can spread to lymph nodes, particularly on the lip, ear and in people on immune-suppressing medication.

Sites
Ear rim, lower lip, scalp, back of hands, forearms, lower legs
Look
Firm rough or crusted lump, often tender; may look wart-like, or open into a shallow ulcer with a raised hard rim
Tempo
Weeks to months; frequently painful, which BCC usually is not
Spread
Low to moderate
Treat
Excision with margins, Mohs for high-risk sites, node assessment and radiotherapy in advanced disease
  • Actinic keratosis — a precursor, not yet cancer: rough sandpapery pink patches on sun-damaged skin, easier to feel than to see. A small percentage progress.
  • Bowen's disease — squamous cell carcinoma in situ: a persistent well-defined scaly red plaque, often on the lower leg, that can be mistaken for psoriasis for years.
  • Keratoacanthoma — a volcano-shaped nodule with a central keratin plug that erupts in weeks; treated as a well-differentiated SCC.
  • Marjolin ulcer — SCC arising in an old burn scar, chronic wound or sinus.

The single most useful non-melanoma rule

Any sore, scab or ulcer on sun-exposed skin that has not fully healed after four weeks should be examined by a doctor. Ordinary wounds heal. Skin cancers cycle — they crust, appear to heal, then break down in the same place.

03 — Group two

Melanoma. A minority of cases, the majority of deaths. Curable when thin.

Melanoma

A cancer of melanocytes, the pigment cells sitting along the base of the epidermis. Melanocytes exist wherever pigment exists, so melanoma can appear on skin never exposed to sun, under a nail, in the eye, and on mucosal surfaces. Subtypes are defined by growth pattern and site.

~70% of melanomas

Superficial spreading melanoma

Spreads sideways within the epidermis for months or years before invading downward — which is exactly why it is catchable. An irregular, asymmetric patch with more than one colour and a notched or fading border.

Sites
Back in men, lower legs in women; any site
Signal
Change. A mole that is enlarging, darkening unevenly, or newly itching or bleeding
Spread
High if thick
~15% — most dangerous pattern

Nodular melanoma

Grows downward from the start with no horizontal phase, so it is thick by the time it is noticed. Often symmetrical, evenly coloured and regular-bordered — meaning it fails the classic ABCDE test entirely.

Look
Firm dome-shaped lump, black, blue-black, or entirely pink; bleeds or ulcerates readily
Tempo
Weeks. Elevation and firmness matter more than colour
Spread
High
~10%

Lentigo maligna melanoma

Arises on chronically sun-damaged skin in older adults. Begins as lentigo maligna, an in-situ stain that widens slowly over 5–20 years before any invasion — often dismissed as an age spot.

Sites
Cheek, temple, nose, ear, neck, forearm
Look
Large flat mottled brown patch with an irregular outline that keeps creeping outward
Spread
Low until invasive
The one most often missed

Acral lentiginous melanoma

Occurs on palms, soles and nail beds. It is not caused by sun exposure, and it accounts for a much larger share of melanomas in people with brown and black skin — where it is frequently found late.

Sites
Sole of the foot, palm, thumb or great toenail
Look
Widening brown-black patch on a sole; or a dark longitudinal nail streak that broadens, or whose pigment spreads to the surrounding skin fold
Spread
High — usually late diagnosis
No pigment

Amelanotic melanoma

Makes little or no pigment, so it appears pink, red or skin-coloured. It is routinely mistaken for a cyst, a scar or an insect bite. Any pink nodule that grows steadily over weeks deserves the same suspicion as a black one.

Look
Pink or flesh-toned nodule, sometimes with a faint brown rim at the base
Spread
High
Uncommon variants

Desmoplastic & mucosal melanoma

Desmoplastic melanoma forms a firm scar-like plaque, often unpigmented, and tends to track along nerves. Mucosal melanoma arises in the mouth, nasal cavity, genital or anal lining, and has no relationship to ultraviolet light. Uveal melanoma of the eye is a separate disease with its own staging.

Spread
High — often diagnosed late
04 — Group three

Tumours of the other cells living in the skin: nerve, immune, vascular and connective tissue.

Rarer skin cancers

Together these make up a small fraction of cases, but several are aggressive and all of them are commonly misread as something harmless on first sight.

Why the rare ones get missed

Almost every tumour in this table first presents as something ordinary — a stye, a bruise, a patch of eczema, a lipoma. The reliable signal is not what it looks like on day one but what it does over time: a benign lesion settles, resolves or stays put; a malignant one keeps changing.

05 — Recognition

Warning signs, and where each rule fails

The mnemonics below are screening aids, not tests. Each one was designed for a specific pattern and misses others, so they are best used together.

A

Asymmetry

One half does not mirror the other if you fold it down the middle.

B

Border

Edges that are notched, scalloped, blurred or bleeding into surrounding skin.

C

Colour

More than one shade within a single lesion — brown, black, red, white, blue-grey.

D

Diameter

Wider than about 6 mm, roughly a pencil eraser. Melanomas can be smaller.

E

Evolving

Any change in size, shape, colour or sensation. The most important letter of the five.

For nodular melanoma

E · F · G

ABCDE misses nodular melanoma, which is often round, one colour and neatly bordered. Use instead: Elevated above the skin, Firm to the touch, and Growing continuously for more than a month.

For people with many moles

The ugly duckling

A person's moles usually resemble one another. The one that looks unlike all its neighbours — darker, larger, differently shaped — is worth more attention than any single feature in isolation.

For Merkel cell carcinoma

A · E · I · O · U

Asymptomatic (painless), Expanding rapidly, Immune suppression, Older than 50, UV-exposed fair skin. Three or more of these on a growing nodule warrant urgent assessment.

Signs that should not wait for a routine appointment

  • A mole or patch that has changed in size, shape or colour over recent weeks or months.
  • A lesion that bleeds on its own, without being knocked or scratched.
  • A sore that has not healed in four weeks, or that heals and reopens in the same spot.
  • Any lump growing steadily and firmly over a few weeks, whatever its colour.
  • A new dark stripe under a fingernail or toenail with no remembered injury — particularly if it is widening or the pigment spreads onto the surrounding skin.
  • A new pigmented spot appearing for the first time after roughly age 40.

Skin tone changes where you should look, not whether you should

Skin cancer occurs in every skin tone. In brown and black skin it appears disproportionately on the palms, soles, nail beds and mucous membranes rather than sun-exposed areas, and pigmented lesions can be harder to distinguish against darker background skin. Later diagnosis, not lower incidence, is the reason for worse outcomes. Examine soles, palms, nails, and the mouth.

06 — Staging

What the numbers after a diagnosis actually measure.

Staging and depth

Staging uses the TNM system: T for the tumour itself, N for lymph nodes, M for distant spread. For melanoma, the T value comes almost entirely from thickness.

Breslow thickness

A measurement in millimetres from the top of the granular layer to the deepest tumour cell, taken from the biopsy. It determines stage, how wide the surgical margin needs to be, and whether a sentinel lymph node biopsy is offered. Ulceration and mitotic rate refine it further.

Risk stratification for keratinocyte carcinoma

BCC and SCC are usually described as low- or high-risk rather than staged numerically. High-risk features include diameter above 2 cm, location on the ear, lip or central face, poorly defined borders, recurrence after previous treatment, perineural invasion, poor differentiation, and immunosuppression.
